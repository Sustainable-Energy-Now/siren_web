WEM ESOO Demand Forecast Bias Tracking

How AEMO's WEM ESOO demand forecasts have compared with what actually happened, by year-ahead horizon.

This page is validation-first: before any archive-wide bias figures are shown, this pipeline's own statistics are checked against AEMO's own published forecast-vs-actual comparison (Table 11 of the 2026 WEM ESOO). The extended section below only appears once that check has run.

What is this page, and how should I read it?

Every year, AEMO (and before it, the IMO) publishes the WEM Electricity Statement of Opportunities — a forecast of how much electricity Western Australia's main grid (the SWIS) will need over the next 5–10 years. Those forecasts feed directly into decisions about how much generation and storage capacity the system needs to keep the lights on. This page asks a simple question: looking back, how close have those forecasts actually been? Have they tended to run too high, too low, or about right — and does that change depending on how far ahead AEMO was forecasting?

A few terms used on this page

  • Operational demand — the electricity actually drawn from the grid, i.e. not counting power generated and used on-site (like rooftop solar). This is the figure the system's reliability planning is built around, and the basis this page compares like-for-like.
  • POE10 / POE50 / POE90 ("probability of exceedance") — AEMO doesn't publish one forecast, it publishes a range. A "10% POE" peak demand is a figure AEMO expects to be exceeded in only 1 year out of 10 (i.e. a fairly extreme, hot-summer case); "50% POE" is the middle-of-the-road case; "90% POE" is the mild-weather, low end of the range.
  • Year-ahead horizon — how many years in advance a forecast was made for. A forecast published in 2020 for the year 2021 is "1 year ahead"; the same publication's forecast for 2025 is "5 years ahead". Forecasts generally get less accurate the further ahead they look, so this page always breaks results down by horizon rather than lumping every forecast together.

Why "validation-first"?

Before trusting any of our own bias statistics across the full archive, we first check that our method reproduces numbers AEMO has already published themselves (Table 11 of the 2026 WEM ESOO, comparing 2025's forecast against what actually happened). If our approach can't reproduce AEMO's own stated numbers for a case where the answer is already known, there's no reason to trust it anywhere else. That check is shown first on this page, above the archive-wide results.

Why does it sometimes say "Insufficient evidence" instead of an answer?

With only a handful of years of data for any given forecast horizon, it's easy to mistake ordinary year-to-year weather variation for a genuine, persistent bias. This page will only call a bias "high" or "low" once there's enough data (at least 5 comparable years) and a statistical test confirms the pattern isn't simply due to chance. Below that bar, it reports "insufficient evidence" rather than guessing — this is a deliberate choice to accrue evidence honestly over time (as the archive grows year on year) rather than publish an early, potentially misleading verdict.

Known limitations, stated plainly

  • The IMO-era (pre-2017) editions used the term "sent out energy" and reported on a basis that isn't confirmed to be identically defined to AEMO's later "operational demand" — the closest available match has been used, with that caveat recorded against every such figure.
  • AEMO's exact convention for which direction counts as an "exceedance" for minimum demand (as opposed to peak demand) hasn't been confirmed — minimum-demand figures are excluded from the calibration check below until that's resolved, rather than guessed.
  • The "extended" section below depends on actual demand computed from real grid metering data; if that hasn't been fully investigated and confirmed accurate for a given period, treat the extended figures for that period with appropriate caution.
